Tankless Recirculation Pumps in Mountlake Terrace: Need One or Not?

You turn on the kitchen tap and wait for hot water that is still coming from the other end of the house. A tankless recirculation pump in Mountlake Terrace can shorten that wait, but only if the layout and the controls fit your habits.

By the Mountlake Terrace Express Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026

Start with the wait, not the pump

A tankless heater makes hot water fast, but it cannot shorten the trip between the heater and your tap. Hot water has to push the cooled water out of the pipe first, and the longer and wider that pipe is, the longer you wait.

A recirculation pump keeps warm water moving, either through a dedicated return line or by using the cold line as the return. Do you need one? If your wait is short, probably not. If a far bathroom or kitchen makes you run the tap for a long time, it can be worth it.

Signs a pump would help

You are a good candidate when most of these fit.

  • ✓The wait is long at one or two fixtures. The rest of the house gets hot water reasonably fast.
  • ✓The heater sits far from those fixtures. A bathroom addition or a second story is a typical case.
  • ✓You use hot water in predictable bursts. Morning showers and evening dishes fit a timer well.
  • ✓You hate running water down the drain. A pump brings hot water to the fixture without waiting at the tap.
  • ✓Your heater can take a pump. Some models have a port or a built-in recirculation mode.

What happens inside the loop

With a dedicated return line, the pump sits at the heater and pulls cooled water back through a second pipe, so warm water is always travelling past the branch to each fixture. When you open a tap, the hot water you need is already a short way off.

A crossover system works differently. A small pump under the farthest sink pushes hot water into the cold line until a sensor says the line is warm, then shuts off. The cold side acts as the return, which is why it needs no new pipe but leaves the cold tap lukewarm for a while.

Either way, the heater has to accept the flow. Some units want a pump wired to the heater's own controls, and some want a separate circuit. A technician reads the model before choosing the parts.

Each approach trades wait time for cost, noise and energy use.

Noise, heat loss and other trade-offs

Pumps hum, and a crossover valve can click or buzz in a cabinet. Mounting the pump on rubber isolators and keeping it off hollow framing helps. A pump that rattles is usually bolted tight to something that amplifies it.

Warm water sitting in a long pipe loses heat. A pump that runs all day makes the heater fire to replace that heat, which costs gas or power for no benefit at 3 a.m. A timer or a button keeps the benefit and trims the waste.

Water hammer can also follow a poor install, such as a pump without a check valve. A good installer sets the flow direction, places the check valve, and tests the loop before leaving.

How to decide, in four steps

You can do the first three before anyone comes to the house.

01

Time the wait

Run the farthest tap and count how long it takes for warm water to arrive. Do the same at the nearest fixture.

02

Check the heater's manual

Look for a recirculation port, a built-in mode, or a statement about adding a pump. If the manual is gone, the model number on the unit is enough.

03

Pick a control style

A push button is cheap to run, a timer covers routines, and a temperature sensor keeps the loop warm when it cools.

04

Call for a layout check

A technician traces the pipe, spots a return path, and tells you if a pump will help. See water heater installation for how we plan a loop.

A pump cannot fix a heater that is too small

If hot water fades during a shower, that is a flow and capacity question, not a wait question. A pump moves hot water around, but it does not make more of it.

That is a different conversation, and it starts with how many fixtures run at the same time.

When to stop and call

  • ✓A gas smell near the heater. Leave the building and call the gas utility or 911, then call us.
  • ✓Water dripping from the pump or the valve. Shut the supply to that branch and call.
  • ✓A heater that shows an error code after you change settings. Do not keep resetting it, call us.
  • ✓Scalding water at the fixtures. A mixing valve or a heater setting may be wrong.

What to ask before anyone drills a hole

Ask where the pump will sit, how it will be supported, and where the check valve goes. Ask what controls it, and if you can change the schedule yourself.

Ask how the install handles the pipe supports, because a pump and a return line add weight and a little vibration to fittings that were built only to carry water. A clear answer here tells you whether the installer has planned the job or is guessing.

Larger additions that put a bathroom at the far end of the house are the ones where a long wait shows up, and the pump decision is worth a closer look.

This is earthquake country, so a wall-mounted tankless unit should sit on solid framing and any pump and return line should be supported rather than hanging from soldered joints.
