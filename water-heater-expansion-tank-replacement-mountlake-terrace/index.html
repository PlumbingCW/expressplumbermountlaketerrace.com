Does Your Mountlake Terrace Water Heater Need a New Expansion Tank?

A dripping relief valve or a thump in the pipes after the heater fires can point to a failed expansion tank. This guide covers what the tank does, how to check it, and when water heater expansion tank replacement in Mountlake Terrace is the right call.

What the tank does and why it fails quietly

Heating water makes it expand. On an open system the extra volume pushes back into the city main and nobody notices. On a closed system, where a check valve or pressure reducing valve blocks that path, the extra volume has nowhere to go, and pressure jumps every time the burner or element fires.

An expansion tank is a small steel vessel with an air cushion on one side of a rubber diaphragm. Water moves into the other side as it expands, then drains back out as it cools. That is the whole job.

So here is the short answer. If your heater sits on a closed system, you want a working tank, and a failed one should be replaced rather than ignored. It fails quietly because hot water keeps coming, while the fittings and the relief valve take the punishment.

Several of these look like other problems, so treat the list as a place to start looking, not a diagnosis.

A safe way to check it yourself

You do not need tools beyond a flashlight and a small screwdriver, and you never need to open anything that holds hot water.

01

Find the tank

It hangs from the cold supply line near the heater, often blue, gray or white, and usually about the size of a football or a small keg. It has a tire-style valve on the bottom or the top.

02

Look before you touch

Check for rust, drips or a puddle on the floor beneath it. A wet base or crusty stem means stop here and call.

03

Tap the shell

A healthy tank sounds hollow near the air side and dull near the water side. If it thuds from top to bottom, the air cushion is probably gone.

04

Press the valve stem briefly

A small puff of air is normal. A spray of water means the diaphragm has failed and the tank is done.

Replace, recharge, or leave it alone

A tank that has only lost air, with an intact diaphragm, can sometimes be recharged. The air side has to be set to match your house pressure, and that reading is only accurate with the water side shut and drained down. Guessing with a tire pump can leave the tank too soft or too hard.

Once water comes out of the valve stem, recharging will not help. The diaphragm is torn and the tank should be replaced. The new one needs to be sized to the heater volume, matched to the house pressure, and supported so it does not hang on the threads alone.

Leaving it alone is reasonable only on an open system, where the tank was never doing anything. That is a judgment made by looking at the valves on the incoming line, not by looking at the tank.

A dripping relief valve is a warning

The temperature and pressure relief valve is the last safety device on the heater. If it drips, something is pushing past its limit. Never plug it, cap it, or tighten it shut, and never run a tube from it to a place where it cannot be seen.

A one-time drip right after a hot shower is a different story from a steady one. If the drip repeats, call before the valve wears out and starts leaking constantly. See our water heater repair page for the other things that can trip that valve.

What this means in Mountlake Terrace

Inside city limits the City of Mountlake Terrace supplies the water, and the supply side often runs through a pressure reducing valve or a backflow device where it enters the house. Those parts can close the system. If one is on your incoming line, an expansion tank is how the heater's extra volume gets somewhere to go, so a failed tank is worth fixing rather than ignoring.

This is earthquake country. A heater should be strapped to the wall framing, and the tank hanging off its cold line adds weight to a threaded fitting. Ask that the tank be braced, and that the strap check happens on the same visit, so one shake does not snap the connection and drain the heater onto the floor.
